Stop losing tabs: a research system that actually holds

Forty tabs is not a system, it's an anxiety. A lightweight capture-inbox-file loop that survives closing the laptop.

S By The editors September 29, 2026 7 min read

Forty tabs is not a system. It is a list of decisions you have been avoiding, rendered as a strip of favicons. The tabs themselves are not the problem — the unresolved "I will deal with this later" is.

This is the lightest version of a research system that survives real life: three buckets, one tripwire, and fifteen minutes on Friday.

Three buckets, no exceptions

Every piece of information you encounter is in one of three states. Give each state a home, and the tab strip stops being the default.

  • Now. What you are working on this hour. Five to seven tabs, maximum. If it is not needed for the current task, it does not belong here.
  • Next. Things you intend to read or watch, but not now. These go straight into a read-later inbox — Pocket, Instapaper, an Are.na channel, a notes file. The tool does not matter. One inbox matters.
  • Someday. Reference material you want to keep. This is a bookmark, not a tab. It survives restarts, syncs across devices, and costs nothing.

The rule that makes this work is simple: a tab can only be in Now. The moment you decide something is for later, it leaves the tab strip. Not after you finish reading one more thing. Immediately.

The tripwire

Pick a number — twelve is a good start — and treat it as a hard limit. When you reach it, stop and triage in under two minutes. Every tab gets one of three fates:

  1. Kill it. You are not going to read it. You know this.
  2. Send it to the read-later inbox with one click, title and all.
  3. Finish it now, because it is actually part of the task in front of you.

The triage is the whole system. Do it badly and often rather than perfectly and rarely. A tab you send to the inbox and never open was going to be a tab you never opened and felt guilty about. The only difference is that one of them is gone.

Permission slip

A library is allowed to contain unread books. Saving something is not a promise to read it — it is a decision to stop carrying it. Most of what you save will go unread, and that is the correct outcome.

The Friday reset, in fifteen minutes

One short session a week is enough to keep the whole thing from silting up. Set a timer.

  1. Minute 0-3. Save every open tab as a session or a list in a note, then close every window. Do not read anything yet.
  2. Minute 3-6. Open the list and promote exactly three items back into tabs. These are your weekend reading, if you want it.
  3. Minute 6-10. Skim the read-later inbox. Delete roughly eighty percent of it without guilt.
  4. Minute 10-14. File the three or four keepers as bookmarks with searchable titles, not just the page title.
  5. Minute 14-15. Empty the trash. Close the laptop.

For projects that run for weeks

Some work genuinely needs twenty tabs at once: a migration, a research paper, a house hunt. Contain it instead of fighting it.

  • One window per project. Close the window when the project ends. This is the most effective habit in this article.
  • Tab groups in Chrome and Edge, or the sidebar in Firefox, for the tabs that must stay loaded.
  • Pinned tabs for the two or three dashboards you check daily. Pinned tabs are small, permanent, and impossible to lose.
  • A session manager for anything longer than a month, so a whole window can be archived and reopened later.

The uncomfortable part

You will not read most of it. The article you saved in March, the documentation for a library you did not adopt, the three versions of the same tutorial — none of that is coming back. Closing it is not failure. Closing it is the feature.

And if something genuinely matters, it will find you again. Search is very good now. The things worth keeping are the ones you will remember a phrase from, and those you can look up in ten seconds.
