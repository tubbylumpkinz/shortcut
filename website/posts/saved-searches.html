The quiet superpower of the saved search

Set it once, get told when the thing you want appears. The most underused button on every marketplace, job board and archive.

S By The editors September 15, 2026 6 min read

Every marketplace, job board and archive has a search box. Almost none of them make the "save this search" button obvious. That button is the most underrated feature on the internet.

A search answers a question once. A saved search answers it every day, without you asking.

What it actually does

A saved search stores your query and runs it on a schedule. Depending on the site, it shows up as a folder in a sidebar, a bookmark, an email digest, or a notification. You stop checking the site; the site starts reporting to you.

The distinction matters more than it sounds. Checking is a slot machine: you pull the lever, mostly get nothing, and slowly train yourself to pull it more often. An alert is a filter that only speaks when something changes.

Where it pays for itself

  • Marketplaces. eBay saved searches can email you when a listing appears. Sort by "newly listed" rather than "ending soon" and you see things before the bidding war starts.
  • Job boards. A saved search with a tight query beats scrolling a feed that is optimised for engagement rather than for you.
  • Real estate. Portals let you save a search with map bounds and price ceilings, then alert on new listings. In a fast market, the alert is the difference between seeing a place and seeing its sold price.
  • Research. Google Scholar, arXiv and PubMed all offer citation and keyword alerts. Set one on the exact phrase your field uses, not the general topic.
  • Code. GitHub lets you save issue and pull-request filters. A query like is:issue is:open no:assignee label:bug saved as a link is a lightweight bug queue.
  • The open web. Google Alerts still works for names, phrases and niche terms. It is quiet, unglamorous, and occasionally the first place you hear about something.
  • Sites without feeds. An RSS bridge can turn a saved search page into a feed, so it lands in the reader you already check.
Auction tip

On any second-hand site, save the search for the exact model name plus an exclusion for the accessories you do not want. Then sort by newest. Most good deals are gone within a day, and the alert arrives before the crowd does.

Write queries that do not drown you

The quality of a saved search is entirely in the query. A broad one will teach you to ignore your own alerts, which is the only real failure mode.

Then constrain the alert itself: daily rather than instant, one channel rather than three, and a folder or label rather than your main inbox. An alert that interrupts is an alert you will turn off within a month.

Five is the limit

Alert fatigue is real and it compounds. If you have twenty saved searches, you have twenty emails you skim and delete, which is worse than having none because it costs attention every day. Keep five, and make each one specific enough that a hit is genuinely interesting.

Once a month, open the list and ask a single question of each entry: did this ever tell me something I acted on? If the answer is no twice in a row, delete it. You can always recreate it when your needs change.

The second-order benefit

After a few months, the alerts become an archive. You learn what a fair price looks like, how often the thing you want actually appears, which sellers are reliable, and what "good" means in a market you thought you understood. That knowledge is worth more than any individual notification.

Patience is much easier when you have a machine watching for you. That is the whole trick: stop checking, start filtering, and let the internet report to you instead.
