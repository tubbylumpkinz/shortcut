A sane privacy setup in one afternoon

Not a threat model, not a lifestyle. Six changes that cut most casual tracking and cost you almost nothing in convenience.

S By The editors October 7, 2026 11 min read

Most privacy advice fails because it arrives as a lifestyle: new phone, new email, new browser, throw away your accounts. Nobody does that. What works is a short list of changes that cost you almost nothing, done once, then revisited occasionally.

This is that list. One afternoon, six changes, no subscriptions required.

First, decide what you are protecting against

"Privacy" is not a goal, it is a direction. Before touching settings, answer three questions honestly:

  1. Who do you not want seeing what? Advertisers, your employer, a partner, a stranger who finds your profile?
  2. What would actually be costly if it leaked? Your address, your messages, your browsing history, your location in real time?
  3. What convenience will you not give up? Be specific. This is the constraint that keeps the plan alive.

Someone protecting a source is solving a different problem from someone who just does not want to be retargeted by a mattress ad for three weeks. Both are valid. The settings are different.

Hour one — the browser

The browser is where most of your online life happens, so it gets most of the budget. Use Firefox, Brave, or Safari; all three ship tracking protection that works without configuration. If you stay on Chrome, expect to do the work manually.

  • Install one blocker. uBlock Origin, or the built-in shields in Brave. One, not four. Multiple blockers mostly slow each other down.
  • Block third-party cookies. In Firefox this is a setting; in Safari it is the default; in Chrome it is buried under privacy settings.
  • Separate your identities. Use Firefox containers, Chrome profiles, or a second browser for shopping and social. This one change breaks a surprising amount of cross-site tracking, because the cookies simply are not in the same jar.
  • Audit your extensions. Open the list and read the permissions. An extension that can read and change all your data on all sites is, functionally, a keylogger you installed on purpose.
Worth repeating

A private window is not anonymity. It forgets local history and logs you out; your ISP still sees the traffic, the site still sees your IP, and your fingerprint is unchanged. It is a clean slate, not a disguise.

Hour two — accounts

Almost every real breach you will experience starts with a password that was reused. Fix that and you have solved the majority of your risk in about forty minutes.

  1. Get a password manager and import everything. Bitwarden, 1Password, Proton Pass, KeePassXC — the differences matter far less than the fact that you actually use it. Turn on its browser extension.
  2. Change the repeats first. Sort by reused passwords and fix those accounts before touching anything else. Then let the manager generate the rest as you log in naturally.
  3. Turn on two-factor, in an app not by SMS. Text messages are better than nothing and worse than almost everything else. Store the recovery codes somewhere offline.
  4. Check what is already out there. Search your address on Have I Been Pwned. For any hit, change that password and anything that shared it.
  5. Add email aliases. A different address for every signup means a breach cannot be correlated across services, and you can switch off an address when the spam starts. SimpleLogin, Firefox Relay and Apple's Hide My Email all do this.

Hour three — the quiet leaks

These are the ones that do not show up in a headline but do change what strangers can find out about you in five minutes.

  • Reset your advertising ID on your phone, then turn off personalised ads. It is not a wall, but it is a speed bump.
  • Review location permissions. Most apps that ask for "while using the app" do not need it. Set photo apps to "never" unless you are actively navigating.
  • Turn off account discovery by phone and email. Otherwise anyone holding your number can find every profile you own.
  • Opt out of the worst data brokers. This is tedious and endless, so only do the three that actually list you. Search your name first; if you are not there, skip it.

What to skip

VPN theatre. A VPN moves trust from your ISP to a company you picked because of a YouTube ad. It is useful on public Wi-Fi and for geo-restricted content. It is not a privacy plan.

Clearing cookies daily. Miserable, and it breaks every site you use. Block third-party cookies instead and leave the first-party ones alone.

Worrying about fingerprinting before you have fixed passwords. The basics protect you from the attacks that actually happen. Start there.

The twenty-minute quarterly ritual

Privacy is maintenance, not a project. Put one reminder in the calendar every three months and do exactly this: re-run the breach check, review app permissions, delete accounts you no longer use, rotate any alias that started getting spam, confirm your recovery codes still exist somewhere physical, and export a fresh encrypted backup of the vault.

That is it. Six changes, four times a year, and you are ahead of nearly everyone you know without having made your life noticeably harder.
