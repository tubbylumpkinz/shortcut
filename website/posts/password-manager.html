Your password manager is doing 20% of its job

If you're only storing passwords, you're missing passkeys, aliases, breach alerts and the vault that makes the whole thing worth it.

S By The editors September 8, 2026 9 min read

If your password manager is only storing passwords, you are using about a fifth of it. The rest of the product — passkeys, aliases, breach monitoring, emergency access — is where the actual security comes from.

None of this requires a better app. It requires turning on the features the app already shipped.

Passkeys, before anything else

A passkey replaces the password with a cryptographic key held by your device. There is nothing to type, nothing to phish, and nothing to reuse. The site stores a public key; the private key never leaves your phone or laptop.

Enable them in this order: your email account, your bank, then the services you would least like to lose — GitHub, your domain registrar, cloud storage. Most major platforms now bury the option under "security" rather than advertising it, so look for it before you assume it is missing.

Two caveats. Passkeys sync with your platform account (iCloud, Google), which is convenient and also a single point of failure. And losing every device means recovering through that platform. Keep the password as a fallback, and keep your account recovery details current.

One email address per service

Your email address is a permanent identifier that you hand to every company you have ever signed up with. When one of them is breached, that address links you to the breach and often to your other accounts.

An alias service fixes this. SimpleLogin, Firefox Relay and Apple's Hide My Email all generate a unique address per signup and forward mail to your real inbox. Benefits compound: spam is traceable to its source, a leak cannot be correlated across sites, and you can switch an address off without losing access to anything else.

If you want the free version: you+shopname@example.com works on many services and gives you filtering, but any determined person can strip the suffix. It is better than nothing, not the same thing.

Second factors belong in the vault, mostly

Storing your TOTP codes in the same vault as your passwords is a real trade-off. It is convenient, it syncs, and it means one breach of the vault defeats both factors. It also protects you from phishing better than SMS ever will.

OptionGood forWeakness
Hardware keyEmail, bank, anything criticalCost, one more thing to lose
Separate authenticator appEverything importantAnother device to back up
TOTP inside the vaultEveryday accountsSingle point of failure
SMS codesLast resort onlySIM swapping, interception

A reasonable setup: hardware keys on your top three accounts, app-based codes for everything valuable, TOTP in the vault for the long tail. What you should not do is keep recovery codes in the same vault as the password that unlocks it.

Rotation is mostly a myth

Changing a strong password every ninety days makes it worse, not better: people add a number, increment it, and reuse the pattern. Rotate on evidence — a breach, a shared password, a service that stores passwords badly — not on a calendar.

Length beats complexity. A four-word passphrase is easier to type and harder to crack than eight characters of punctuation. And the one password that genuinely deserves paranoia is the one you never type into a website: the master password for the vault.

Emergency access is the feature nobody configures

Every manager has some version of this: a trusted contact who can request access after a waiting period, an encrypted export, a printable recovery kit. Set it up anyway. The failure mode is not exotic — it is a hospital, a move, or a dead laptop, and it leaves someone you love locked out of the accounts that matter.

Keep the recovery kit somewhere physical and boring. A sealed envelope in a drawer beats a note in the same cloud account you are trying to recover.

The twenty-minute audit

Open the vault and walk through five filters: reused passwords, weak passwords, passwords older than five years, accounts without two-factor, and logins for services you no longer use. Fix the first two, enable second factors on anything financial, and delete the dead accounts. Then close it for another quarter.

That is the whole job. The manager is not a filing cabinet for passwords; it is the control panel for your online identity. Most people never open the panel.
